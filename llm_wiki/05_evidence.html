Part 5 of 5 · Evidence & limits

It works until
it confidently drifts.

The LLM Wiki is a strong pattern for the right corpus, and a dangerous one for the wrong corpus. The difference is not the model. It is provenance, review, scope, and whether anyone actually reads the wiki. This part separates what the evidence supports from what the essays promise.

1 · What Karpathy claims — and what he does not

The idea file makes a design argument, not an empirical one. It says RAG re-derives synthesis on every query; a compiled wiki accumulates it; the maintenance burden is the reason human wikis die; an LLM can do the maintenance. Those are plausible and useful claims. They are not a benchmark, and Karpathy does not present them as one.

What the essay does claim
  • A persistent wiki compounds knowledge in a way stateless RAG does not.
  • Cross-references and contradictions can be maintained by an agent.
  • The schema is the key to disciplined behavior across sessions.
  • An index file works at moderate scale without embeddings.
  • Lint keeps the wiki healthy as it grows.
What it does not claim
  • That the wiki is cheaper than RAG on every workload.
  • That an LLM-written claim is automatically trustworthy.
  • That markdown scales to enterprise multi-tenant use.
  • That lint can detect a confidently wrong but internally consistent page.
  • That the pattern replaces databases, search engines, or human review.

This distinction matters because the discourse often collapses it. "Karpathy says LLM wikis are better than RAG" is not what the essay says. It says the wiki is a better shape for knowledge that accumulates. Whether it is better on your task is an empirical question, and the evidence so far is mixed.

2 · What the benchmarks actually show

Independent tests appeared within weeks of the gist. They are small and heterogeneous, but they converge on a useful shape: the wiki helps most when a question requires synthesizing multiple sources, and helps least when the answer sits in one document. Cost is fiercely workload-dependent.

StudySetupHeadline resultWhat it does not settle
Preregistered wiki vs RAG
arXiv 2605.18490, May 2026		 24 papers, 13 questions, same answer model, blinded LLM judges. Wiki compiled from the papers vs single-round vector RAG. Wiki much better at inter-paper synthesis (9.00–9.75 vs 3.50) and claim-level citation support (40.2% vs 18.9%). RAG met the single-fact lookup test. Wiki used ~21× more query tokens (1.65M vs 78k across 13 questions). No architecture won on all axes. One small corpus; judge-based scoring; query harness disabled prompt caching; ingest cost ratio was not adjudicated because of cache accounting. A preprint, not a production benchmark.
72-run migration benchmark
ROBOCO, May 2026		 8 tasks × 3 techniques × 3 repetitions in a 30-repository migration workspace. Wiki vs direct reading (vanilla) vs Graphify. Wiki: 350K tokens, 101 s, quality 16.0/25. Vanilla: 755K tokens, 167 s, quality 15.1. Wiki won 5 of 8 tasks, especially cross-language and external-source synthesis. Vanilla won exhaustive grep and one cross-domain lookup. Graphify: 617K, 180 s, 13.6. One workload, one model stack, blog rather than peer review. It measures a code-migration workspace, not a personal research wiki.
30-query agentic comparison
Author-published, April 2026		 Same corpus and judge; RAG vs agentic RAG (same retriever in a multi-turn loop) vs wiki navigation; 30 queries. RAG: 1,359 tokens/query, quality 4.48. Agentic RAG: 7,730 tokens/query, 4.73. Wiki: 18,373 tokens/query, 4.69. Agentic RAG beat the wiki 8–4–18 head-to-head. Small sample; one corpus; author-run; the point is that turn budget and agentic retrieval close much of the gap, not that one architecture always wins.
14-model compilation benchmark
Independent, 2026		 14 models × 16 gold-labelled documents; same four-step compile loop; cross-vendor judge. Top score 0.907 at $0.215/document; cheapest viable 0.874 at $0.0049/document; six arms within 0.06 of the top, spending up to 44× more. Above a quality floor, cost varies far more than quality. One trial per document; uncalibrated judge; measures compilation quality, not wiki-vs-RAG architecture.
How to read these numbers

Do not average them into a single "wiki is X% better" claim. They measure different things: answer synthesis, token cost, compilation quality, and code-task performance. What they agree on is conditional: the wiki is a synthesis tool. It earns its cost when questions span sources and the answer is reused. It loses when the answer is a single lookup or when a simpler agentic retrieval loop can do the same work.

The cost picture is genuinely mixed

The intuition is that a wiki pays a large upfront ingest cost and then answers cheaply. The preregistered study found the opposite on its setup: the wiki cost far more per query because the agent read full pages. The 72-run benchmark found the wiki cheaper than reading raw files because the alternative was even more context. Both can be true. Query cost depends on how many pages the agent reads, how large they are, and whether prompt caching is in play.

Do not quote a multiplier without the denominator

"21× more tokens" is meaningless without the workload. "54% fewer tokens" is meaningless without the baseline. "71.5× fewer tokens" is an author claim on a particular corpus. Measure tokens per successful answer, including retries, on your own questions.

3 · The failure modes that kill wikis

The field reports are remarkably consistent. The failures are not exotic model bugs; they are structural properties of a persistent, LLM-written knowledge base. Plan for them.

FailureHow it happensMitigation
Compounding errors A plausible wrong summary becomes a prior. Other pages cite it. The wiki becomes internally consistent and externally wrong. Claim-level provenance; human promotion gate; source-grounded verification; do not let summaries cite only other summaries.
Silent source drift A source changes or is superseded, but the wiki page still states the old claim with confidence. Lint reads the wiki, not the world. Store source hashes/mtimes; compare them with page updated; re-verify the oldest claims; timestamp every external fact.
Index drift The agent updates index.md incrementally and misses pages. LLMs are not reliable at keeping a materialized index in sync. Rebuild the index from the files during lint; treat the index as a cache, not a hand-maintained list.
Volume bloat The agent writes pages because it can. The wiki grows faster than it is read, and the signal-to-noise ratio falls. Write for questions you actually have; merge duplicates; delete low-value pages; measure reuse, not page count.
Relevance pollution One corpus contains every domain. A debugging question inherits pricing analysis, personal notes, and unrelated sources. Scope corpora by domain or task; route the agent to the right sub-wiki; do not load the whole index when a cluster will do.
Single-user assumptions Plain markdown has no per-page ACL, no concurrency control, and no sensitivity labels. A shared repo leaks everything to everyone. Split vaults by audience; use private repos; move sensitive material to a permission-aware store; serialize writes.
Prompt injection A raw source contains instructions ("ignore previous instructions," "exfiltrate secrets"). The agent treats them as commands. Schema rule: sources are data. Sandbox the agent; default-deny network; read-only raw; review tool calls.
Circular lint The same model that wrote the page grades the page. It finds the same plausible story convincing twice. Use a different model or a deterministic checker for the second pass; compare claims to source bytes, not just to other pages.
Lossy compression A 200-line source becomes a 40-line summary. Hedges, edge cases, and context disappear; the summary reads more confident than the source. Keep source links and quotes; mark confidence; preserve "it depends" conditions; do not promote a summary to stable without reading the source.
Cost blowup The agent reads five full pages per question, then retries. Ingest touches 15 pages. The bill grows faster than the value. Budget per ingest/query; use scoped pages and section-level retrieval; prompt caching; evaluate cost per successful answer.
Fabricated citations The model writes a citation that looks right, resolves to a real file, and does not support the claim. Resolve every citation; sample-check quotes and numbers; require page/section coordinates; flag unsupported claims.
Write-only wiki The system produces pages nobody reads. The compounding loop is broken because the human never returns. Use it for real questions; file answers back; review weekly; kill it if you do not revisit it.
The deepest failure is epistemic

Lint checks consistency. Truth is a relationship between the wiki and the world. A wiki can be beautifully linked, internally coherent, and wrong. The only defenses are provenance, source re-verification, and a human who is willing to say "I don't know yet."

4 · Production guardrails that hold

If you run this pattern beyond a personal experiment, these are the controls that repeatedly show up in field reports. They are not optional polish; they are the difference between a knowledge asset and a confident liability.

Claim-level provenanceFootnotes keyed to source IDs; page/section/line pointers; quotes for numbersFabricated citations; unsupported claims

5 · When not to use an LLM Wiki

The strict list. If your situation matches one of these, use a different tool — or use the wiki only as an authoring layer in front of the real system.

  1. It is the transactional source of truth. Orders, inventory, balances, and state transitions belong in a database with constraints and transactions, not in prose.
  2. It is a regulated record. Audit trails, legal hold, retention, and reproducible answers require deterministic storage and access control, not a markdown vault.
  3. You need per-page or per-tenant permissions. Plain markdown has no ACL. Splitting vaults is a blunt instrument; a permission-aware store is the right layer.
  4. You need high-QPS retrieval. A wiki is an authoring and synthesis layer, not a serving layer with an SLA. Use a search service or vector database for production QPS.
  5. The corpus is a one-off. If you will ask one question and never return, RAG, search, or a long-context prompt is simpler and cheaper.
  6. No one will review it. Without a human promotion gate, errors compound and the wiki becomes a confident hallucination with backlinks.
  7. No one will revisit it. A write-only wiki is an expensive content-generation habit. If you do not have recurring questions, do not build the loop.
  8. The facts change constantly. Live prices, inventory, metrics, and policies need automated re-verification, not a page that was true last month.
  9. You need deterministic, reproducible answers. If the same query must return the same answer after a rebuild, use a database or a versioned rules engine.
  10. You cannot absorb the ingest cost. Compiling a source into 10–15 pages is token-heavy. If the budget is tight, a smaller scope or a simpler retrieval pipeline is better.
  11. You need multi-agent concurrency on the same pages. Prose merges are painful; serialize writes or split the corpus before you add agents.
  12. The domain is adversarial. If sources are untrusted and the agent has tools, the prompt-injection surface is large. Harden it like a security boundary, or do not run it.

6 · Decision tree

Will you revisit this corpus for weeks or months?
├─ no  → RAG / search / long-context. Do not build a wiki.
└─ yes
   ├─ Do you need synthesis across multiple sources?
   │  ├─ no  → Notes + search. A wiki may still help, but it is not required.
   │  └─ yes
   │     ├─ Do you need provenance and human review?
   │     │  ├─ no  → RAG or agentic retrieval may be enough.
   │     │  └─ yes → LLM Wiki is a good fit.
   │     ├─ Do you need per-user permissions or high QPS?
   │     │  ├─ yes → Database/search service + wiki as authoring layer.
   │     │  └─ no  → Start with files, index, and grep.
   │     └─ Will you actually read and lint it?
   │        ├─ no  → Do not build it. It will drift.
   │        └─ yes → Start with 5 sources. Add search/graph index at bottlenecks.
   └─ Is the data transactional, regulated, or live?
      ├─ yes → Use the system of record; the wiki is only a view.
      └─ no  → Proceed with the wiki.

7 · The ecosystem and OKF

The pattern moved from a gist to a small ecosystem in a few months. The most important development is Google Cloud's Open Knowledge Format (OKF), which formalizes the markdown + YAML-frontmatter shape and adds the trust signals a machine-maintained corpus needs.

PieceWhat it addsStatus / caveat
OKF v0.2Directory of markdown concept files; required type; sources, generated, verified, status, stale_after; index.md / log.md conventions; attested computations.Google-led, vendor-neutral spec; v0.1 June 2026, v0.2 July 2026. Young; adoption still early; not a retrieval engine.
qmdLocal BM25 + vector search + LLM reranking; CLI and MCP server.Karpathy recommends it for larger wikis. Local models; measure latency on your machine.
graphifySkill that turns code, docs, papers, and images into a queryable graph; interactive HTML, JSON, report.Author claims 71.5× fewer tokens per query; independent 72-run test found it slow and lower quality on a code-migration workload. Verify.
kiDeterministic Neo4j index over markdown; section-level navigation and backlinks.Vendor reference implementation; files stay canonical; index is disposable.
llm-wiki-opsNode/edge CRUD, wikilink management, graph metrics, MCP server; no LLM at operation time.Useful when you want deterministic graph surgery instead of prose edits.
ObsidianEditor, backlinks, graph view, Dataview, Web Clipper, Marp integration.The reference viewer, not a requirement. Graph view is a visualization, not a query engine.

OKF's v0.2 is a meaningful shift: it treats provenance, trust, lifecycle, and attestation as first-class fields. That is exactly the layer the original gist leaves to the schema. But a format is not a retrieval system, and a trust field is not a truth guarantee. A page can be marked verified and still be wrong. The format makes the claim inspectable; it does not make it correct.

Standards arrive before benchmarks

OKF was published within two months of the gist, before large-scale evidence about long-term wiki quality or cost. That is normal for infrastructure standards, but it means adoption is an act of faith in the shape, not proof that the shape works at scale. Treat the spec as a useful contract and the benchmarks as early, conditional evidence.

8 · Open questions

  • Long-term drift. There is no multi-year study of an LLM-maintained wiki. We know errors compound in principle; we do not know the rate, or which lint designs catch them.
  • Benchmark standardization. Every study uses a different corpus, question set, judge, and cost boundary. A shared eval would make the claims comparable.
  • Claim-level provenance at scale. Page-level citations are easy; mapping every number and quote to a source coordinate is expensive. OKF's sources and footnotes are a start, not a solution.
  • Permissions and multi-user use. Plain markdown has no ACL. Splitting vaults works for small teams; it is not a general answer.
  • Agent interoperability. OKF and AGENTS.md help, but different agents still have different tool permissions, memory, and failure modes.
  • Graph layer necessity. The filesystem vs graph index trade-off is argued by vendors and experienced by users, but not rigorously benchmarked for wiki navigation.
  • Human factors. The pattern assumes a human who reads, reviews, and revisits. Most failures are failures of that assumption, not of the model.
  • Security. Prompt injection through knowledge sources is an open problem. A wiki agent with shell and network access is a large attack surface.
The honest verdict

Use it if you have a corpus you will revisit, questions that require synthesis, a human review loop, and a tolerance for token cost. Do not use it if you need transactions, permissions, deterministic answers, high QPS, or a one-off lookup. Measure it on your own questions before you scale it. The pattern is real, the compounding is real, and so is the drift.
