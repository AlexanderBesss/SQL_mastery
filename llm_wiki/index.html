The wiki that
maintains itself.

Andrej Karpathy's LLM Wiki is not a new database and not a new file format. It is an operating pattern for a personal knowledge base: immutable raw sources, an LLM-maintained collection of interlinked markdown pages, and a written schema that tells the agent how to ingest, answer, and lint. The graph is what you get when ordinary links stop being decoration and become the navigation layer.

Verdicts up front
  1. The pattern is the technology. Karpathy's April 2026 idea file describes three layers — raw sources, an LLM-written wiki, and a schema such as AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md — plus three loops: ingest, query, and lint. There is no runtime to install. The wiki is just a git repository of markdown files.
  2. Karpathy popularized the pattern; he did not invent linked markdown. Wikilinks, Zettelkasten, Obsidian's graph view, and Vannevar Bush's Memex all predate it. What is new is the division of labour: the human curates sources and asks questions, the LLM does the cross-referencing, filing, and bookkeeping that humans abandon.
  3. The important shift is compile-once, query-many. RAG re-derives synthesis from raw chunks on every question. An LLM Wiki compiles the synthesis into pages once, then keeps those pages current. That is why the knowledge compounds — and why errors compound too.
  4. "Markdown as a graph" is real, but it is a derived graph. Files are nodes, links are directed edges, folders are containment, and frontmatter can carry typed relationships. The files remain the source of truth; the graph is an index or a view, not necessarily a second database.
  5. Use it for synthesis, not for transactions. This is a personal or small-team knowledge tool: research, reading, due diligence, course notes, internal wikis. It is a poor fit for regulated records, high-QPS retrieval, multi-tenant permissions, or any corpus you will not actually revisit.

What the thing actually is

Karpathy's gist is deliberately short and deliberately abstract. It describes a persistent wiki that sits between you and your raw documents. When a new source arrives, the agent does not merely index it for later retrieval. It reads the source, extracts the claims, updates entity and concept pages, adds cross-references, notes contradictions with older material, updates the index, and appends to a log. A single source might touch ten or fifteen pages.

At question time you ask the wiki, not the raw pile. The agent reads the index, opens the relevant pages, and synthesizes an answer with citations. If the answer is good, it can be filed back as a new page. The next question starts from a richer artifact than the last one. That is the compounding loop.

"Obsidian is the IDE; the LLM is the programmer; the wiki is the codebase."

Andrej Karpathy, LLM Wiki idea file, 4 April 2026

That metaphor is the clearest way to understand the stack. Markdown files are source code. Links are imports. The index is the table of contents. Git is the history. The agent is the maintainer who will actually keep the imports from rotting. Obsidian is one possible viewer, not a requirement.

Personal research over weeks or months; 10–500 sources; you want synthesis, not just search LLM Wiki + Obsidian + git Compiled pages carry cross-references and contradictions forward. The human curates; the agent does the bookkeeping.
Reading a book or taking a course; each chapter adds characters, themes, or concepts LLM Wiki, ingest one chapter at a time Entity pages and concept pages accumulate. By the end you have a companion wiki, not a pile of summaries.
Team internal wiki fed by Slack threads, meeting transcripts, and project docs LLM Wiki + review gate + scoped corpora The agent can draft freely, but promotion to trusted status needs a human. Split by audience before permissions become a problem.
One-off Q&A over thousands of PDFs or web pages Hybrid RAG (BM25 + vectors + reranking) You do not need a persistent synthesis layer if you will not revisit the corpus. Chunk-and-retrieve is cheaper to stand up.
Exact lookup, audit trail, or regulated record The source of truth plus deterministic queries The wiki is a navigation and synthesis layer. It must cite the raw source; it must not become the authority for a number.
Questions are mostly "what links to this, what is the shortest path, what is central?" Graph index over the markdown (e.g. ki) or a graph database Backlinks and multi-hop traversal are cheap when the graph is indexed. The files stay canonical; the index is disposable.
Wiki has grown past a few hundred pages or many agents query it concurrently Local search (qmd) or a graph index; scope the corpus index.md plus grep becomes linear work. Section-level retrieval and backlink indexes cut the context you load.
High-QPS serving, transactional consistency, per-tenant permissions A database or search service, not this pattern Plain markdown has no access control, no transactions, and no serving SLA. Use the right layer for the product.

Myth check

What this is — and is not
Myth"Karpathy invented a way to store markdown as a graph."
Reality: he popularized a pattern for an LLM-maintained wiki whose links form a graph. Linked notes, wikis, Zettelkasten, and graph views existed long before 2026. His contribution is the maintenance loop and the human/agent split.
Myth"It is a graph database."
Reality: the canonical store is a folder of files. The graph is a projection: links, folders, headings, and optional frontmatter. A graph database can index that projection, but it is not required.
Myth"It replaces RAG."
Reality: it changes when synthesis happens. RAG retrieves and synthesizes per query; the wiki compiles synthesis ahead of time. Agentic RAG can close much of the quality gap in some tests.
Myth"It is cheaper than RAG."
Reality: not automatically. Ingest burns tokens, and a wiki query may read several full pages. One preregistered study measured a ~21× query-token premium; another benchmark measured savings versus reading raw files. Measure your workload.
Myth"It is just Obsidian."
Reality: Obsidian is a viewer and graph browser. The pattern is the raw/wiki/schema structure plus ingest, query, and lint. You can run it in VS Code, a terminal, or any editor.
Myth"The LLM writes everything, so you can forget about it."
Reality: the LLM writes the pages, but humans curate sources, review promotions, and decide what matters. Unreviewed output is a draft, not evidence.

How to read the evidence

Karpathy's essay is a design argument, not a benchmark. The independent tests that followed are small, heterogeneous, and often author-published. They agree on the shape of the trade-off — the wiki helps most when a question requires synthesizing several sources, and helps least when the answer sits in one document — but they disagree on token cost. Treat every number as conditional on corpus, model, prompt, and how much of the wiki the agent reads. Part 5 lays out the benchmarks and their caveats in detail.

Scope and method

This is an engineering investigation, not a product review or a tutorial in prompt magic. Research window: October 2026. Primary sources checked directly: Karpathy's llm-wiki.md gist; Google Cloud's OKF announcement, v0.2 post, and specification; the qmd, graphify, ki, and llm-wiki-ops repositories; and the preregistered wiki-vs-RAG study. Secondary sources include independent benchmarks, field reports, and critiques; they are labeled where they matter.

The report is also an answer to a common misreading. Karpathy did not invent a graph file format, and the pattern is not "put your notes in a graph database." It is a way to make an agent maintain a body of prose that happens to be navigable as a graph. The distinction matters: it tells you what to optimize (maintenance and provenance) and what not to buy (a graph engine you do not need).

The sibling reports in this repository — vector databases and graph databases — cover the retrieval and graph-engine layers in depth. This one sits one level up: how to build a durable knowledge artifact with an agent, and when that artifact is the right tool at all.
