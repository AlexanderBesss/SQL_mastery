What a "graph database"
actually is.

Strip the branding and a graph database does one job: store connections as first-class objects and follow them fast. Whether that beats your existing relational engine is decided by two numbers — how deep your queries walk and how wide they branch — and by whether you are asking a transactional question or an analytical one.

1 · The labeled property graph (and RDF)

Two data models dominate "graph" work. The labeled property graph (LPG) — nodes and directed edges, each with a label/type and arbitrary key–value properties — is what Neo4j, Memgraph, TigerGraph, Neptune (property mode), and Apache AGE implement. The RDF model expresses everything as subject–predicate–object triples with URIs, is W3C-standardized, and is queried with SPARQL; its home is semantic-web / knowledge-base work (GraphDB, Stardog, AllegroGraph, Jena).

DimensionLabeled property graphRDF
Basic unitNode and edge, both carry labels + propertiesTriple (s, p, o); literals or URIs
Edge propertiesFirst-class: (a)-[:WORKS_FOR {since: 2019}]->(b)Awkward: reification or RDF-star; each attribute can become its own triple
Modeling biasVertex-centric; fold attributes into nodes/edges → fewer elementsEverything is a node/triple → more elements, but URIs link across datasets
AssumptionClosed world: absence is usually "false"Open-world: absence is "unknown"; reasoning (RDFS/OWL) can infer facts
LanguageCypher / GQL / Gremlin / GSQL / openCypherSPARQL
Typical homeFraud, recommendations, IT/ops, access, appsGovernment/biomedical knowledge bases, interoperability, ontologies

Rule of thumb: an LPG property that RDF would need a triple for is the practical difference. The "same fact, fewer elements" property is why app teams on a transactional workload pick LPG, while cross-org knowledge interchange and inference push toward RDF. Both are "graphs"; picking the wrong model for the job is a separate mistake from picking wrong between graph and relational.

2 · Index-free adjacency: where the speed claim really lives

A relational engine answers a connection by joining: look up foreign keys in a B-tree (≈ O(log n) per probe), intersect sets, and pay again for every hop. A native graph engine stores each node's incident relationships as direct references, so following an edge is a pointer dereference — the classic fetch-execute primitive. This is index-free adjacency: the cost of a local traversal depends on the degree of the nodes you touch, not the total number of nodes in the graph.

OperationRelational (indexed JOIN)Native traversal (index-free adjacency)
One hopO(log n) B-tree probe per row matched~O(1) pointer dereference per incident edge
k hopsCost compounds per JOIN; grows with table sizeGrows with the branching frontier (Σ degrees), largely independent of graph size
Whole-scan of an attributeB-tree/GIN index is the right toolYou still need a label/property index to find the starting node
Vendor framing, decoded

The strongest version of this claim comes from Neo4j, and it is a vendor claim: "…traversals at cost O(1) per hop… non-native systems pay global-index lookups O(log N)… 6–15× more I/O for even modest graphs," alongside "50 M traversals/sec, 1 M writes/sec, over a trillion records." Directionally this is standard database theory — pointer chase beats B-tree probe per hop — and the ACM Computing Surveys taxonomy ("Demystifying Graph Databases", 2023) describes the same adjacency-list/direct-pointer design space without the marketing. It only matters once your queries actually chain hops; see §3.

Two ways index-free adjacency bites back
  • Supernodes. A node with millions of edges (a celebrity account, a default "unknown customer") makes every traversal through it O(degree). Every graph engine needs special handling here; every graph model should avoid creating them accidentally.
  • Cycle-rich variable-length paths. A pattern like (a)-[*1..6]->(b) on a cyclic graph enumerates combinatorially many edge-unique paths. Apache AGE's own regression suite has a 5-vertex cycle-rich graph that yields ~7,000 undirected paths for [*1..∞] — expected openCypher semantics, explosive cost.

3 · OLTP traversal vs OLAP analytics: two different products

People say "we need graph analytics" and mean one of two opposite things:

  • Operational (OLTP) traversal. Start at a known node, explore a bounded neighborhood, answer "who/what is connected to this, within N hops?" — sub-millisecond to a few ms, high concurrency, point reads/writes. This is what transactional graph databases (Neo4j, Memgraph, Neptune Database, AGE) are built for.
  • Analytical (OLAP) whole-graph computation. PageRank, weakly connected components, Louvain/Leiden communities, BFS/SSSP/CDLP over every vertex — minutes-to-hours batch or near-real-time analytics, not per-request. This is what graph analytics platforms (Neo4j GDS, TigerGraph, Neptune Analytics) and in-process libraries (NetworkX, Graph-tool, igraph) are for.

Decide which question you're answering before picking an engine, because the failure mode of the whole category is buying an OLTP store and running OLAP scans on it (or shipping an app whose "analytics" is a full-graph query on the request path). A single-node graph database is not a distributed analytics system, and an analytics cluster is not your login-path OLTP store.

Standards are real now — read the fine print

In 2024 ISO published GQL (a full database language for property graphs, mostly Cypher-shaped), and 2023 added SQL/PGQ to the SQL standard (property graphs as read-only views over relational tables). So "vendor lock-in via language" is weaker than it was — Cypher skills transfer to GQL, and SQL/PGQ is standard SQL. But conformance is uneven: Neo4j supports "most mandatory" GQL features, AGE is an openCypher subset (see Part 2), and "supports GQL" on a vendor page ≠ your queries port verbatim. Read the conformance matrix, not the press release.

5 · Native vs non-native: a marketing axis worth decoding

"Native graph" is mostly a Neo4j-coined marketing term for "we built our own storage engine around index-free adjacency, so hops are pointer chases." The kernel of truth: the storage layout matters, and bolt-on graph layers over a general storage engine pay more per hop. The honest counterpoints:

  • Relational engines are graph engines with a different layout. Postgres with indexed FKs is a graph store queried via joins; ltree, recursive CTEs, and pgRouting do genuine graph work on genuinely relational storage. DuckDB's DuckPGQ (CIDR 2023) reports competitive graph-query performance in an embedded OLAP engine.
  • Apache AGE is "non-native" by design — it maps Cypher onto Postgres tables and Postgres indexes. It wins on integration and exactness; it can lose on very deep traversals, which is a fair, disclosed tradeoff (Part 2 §5), not a defect to hide.
  • ArangoDB, ArcadeDB, and multi-model stores explicitly hybrid: adjacency structures + general indexes, trading some hop cost for multi-model convenience.

Don't filter vendors on "native". Filter on: hop cost at your depth, concurrency, memory footprint, operational model, licensing, and whether your data can live there as the source of truth.

6 · Evaluation: how to measure graphs honestly (LDBC)

Graph queries are exact — there is no recall dial like ANN has. The axes are latency-vs-hop-depth, throughput, memory, and correctness. The neutral harnesses:

  1. LDBC Social Network Benchmark (SNB) + LSQB: pattern-matching / interactive queries over a synthetic social graph at scale factors (SF1 ≈ 3.9 M vertices / 17.9 M edges upward). Measures the OLTP side.
  2. LDBC Graphalytics: six whole-graph algorithms (PageRank, WCC, SSSP, LCC, BFS, CDLP) on standard datasets. Measures the OLAP side.
  3. Your own workload: your degree distribution, your hop depths, your filters, your write pattern. Load a real slice and run your three worst queries. This beats any published table.
This category's benchmarks are propaganda

Almost every circulating graph-database number is self-published by the vendor it flatters: TigerGraph "2×–8000× faster" (2018 report), Memgraph "up to 41× lower latency / 3–8× faster", ArcadeDB "first on every Graphalytics algorithm" with an unspecified "Neo4j 2026". They agree on one thing (architecture and depth matter) and disagree on everything else. If a decision hangs on a number, run LDBC or your own data — or don't cite the number.
